## Module 5: Other Ajnas Types — Trichords and Pentachords

### 📌 Quick Reference

| Jins | Type | Notes | Quarter-Tone Formula |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sikah** | Trichord (3 notes) | E𝄳 – F – G | 3 – 4 |
| **Ajam** | Pentachord (5 notes) | C – D – E – F – G | 4 – 4 – 2 – 4 |
| **Nawa Athar / Nikriz** | Pentachord (5 notes) | C – D – E♭ – F♯ – G | 4 – 2 – 6 – 2 |

*(A trichord spans a 3rd; a tetrachord — Module 2 — spans a 4th; a pentachord spans a 5th, always totaling 14 quarter-tones = 700 cents.)*

### 5.1 Why Some Ajnas Are Shorter or Longer

In Module 2, all four core ajnas happened to be tetrachords (4 notes). That's the most common size — but not the only one. Traditional theory recognizes two other sizes:

| Type | Notes | Spans | Why this size? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Trichord** | 3 | A 3rd | The jins's mood is fully conveyed in just 3 notes — a 4th note would add nothing essential |
| **Tetrachord** | 4 | A 4th | The default, most common size |
| **Pentachord** | 5 | A 5th | The jins contains an internal feature (often a wide "augmented 2nd" step) that needs a 5th note to resolve cleanly |

Knowing *why* a jins is classified a certain size — not just memorizing the count — will help you recognize these shapes by ear rather than by rote.

### 5.2 Jins Sikah — A Trichord Built on a Note You Already Know

You met **E𝄳 (Sikah)** all the way back in Module 1 as a single neutral pitch. Now meet it as the **root of its own jins**:

| Degree | Note | Interval from previous | Quarter-tones |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | E𝄳 | — | — |
| 2 | F | 3/4 tone | 3 |
| 3 | G | Whole tone | 4 |

**Why only 3 notes?** Because Jins Sikah's defining color — the **neutral 3rd** between E𝄳 and G — is fully expressed in that span. A 4th note doesn't add new character; it's considered optional "extra" material (traditionally called **jins baggage** — decorative notes surrounding the jins's core scale that are common in melodic use but not part of its defining shape).

**Character:** Delicate, contemplative, considered one of the most distinctive-sounding colors in the system precisely because it has no direct Western analogue at all — not even an approximate one, unlike Rast or Bayati.

#### 🔹 Case Study: Maqam Sikah

| Degree | Note | Jins role |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | E𝄳 | Root of **Jins Sikah** (lower) |
| 2 | F | |
| 3 | G | Top of lower jins — **shared note** (Ittisal) |
| 3/4 | G | Root of **Jins (Upper) Rast** — Ghammaz |
| 5 | A | |
| 6 | B𝄳 | |
| — | *(octave continues: C – D – E𝄳)* | |

**Full scale:** E𝄳 – F – G – A – B𝄳 – C – D – E𝄳

**Structure:** Jins Sikah (E𝄳) + Ittisal (shared G) + Jins Upper Rast (G) = **Maqam Sikah**

🎻 **Violin note:** Because the tonic itself (E𝄳) is a quarter-tone pitch, this maqam is genuinely challenging to tune on a Western-strung violin — a good "stretch" piece once Modules 1–4 feel comfortable.

📚 *Reference: [maqamworld.com/en/maqam/sikah.php](https://www.maqamworld.com/en/maqam/sikah.php)*

### 5.3 Jins Ajam — A Pentachord That Sounds Completely Familiar

| Degree | Note | Interval from previous | Quarter-tones |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | C | — | — |
| 2 | D | Whole tone | 4 |
| 3 | E | Whole tone | 4 |
| 4 | F | Semitone | 2 |
| 5 | G | Whole tone | 4 |

**Character:** This is, note-for-note, the **bottom five notes of a Western major scale**. If you can play a major-scale opening, you already know Jins Ajam. No quarter-tones anywhere.

> 💡 **Shortened versions:** Melodies often use just the first 3 notes of this shape as a compact **Ajam trichord** (e.g., F–G–A) or, starting one step higher, a **Jiharkah trichord** (also whole–whole, but functioning on a different scale degree — same shape, different structural role). This is a good example of why *function*, not just *sound*, determines a jins's name.

### 5.4 Jins Nawa Athar (also called Nikriz) — A Dramatic Pentachord

| Degree | Note | Interval from previous | Quarter-tones |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | C | — | — |
| 2 | D | Whole tone | 4 |
| 3 | E♭ | Semitone | 2 |
| 4 | F♯ | **Augmented 2nd** | 6 |
| 5 | G | Semitone | 2 |

**Why a pentachord?** Notice the augmented 2nd (E♭ → F♯) — the same dramatic leap found in Jins Hijaz. In Hijaz, that leap resolves after just one more note (F♯ → G, a tetrachord). Here, an **additional preceding step** (C → D) is considered structurally essential to the jins's identity, pushing it to 5 notes total.

**Character:** Intense, ornate, "Hungarian minor"–like to Western ears. Rarely used to build an entire independent piece — more often appears as a striking splash of color during modulation (recall **Tanjees** from Module 4).

#### 🔹 Case Study: Maqam Nikriz (Nawa Athar)

**Full scale:** C – D – E♭ – F♯ – G – A♭ – B – C

**Structure:** The Nawa Athar pentachord (C→G) forms the root, continuing upward with a further tetrachord to complete the octave. Because Nikriz's continuation varies somewhat between regional traditions, always confirm the exact upper notes against a primary source before teaching it as fixed: 📚 *[maqamworld.com/en/maqam/nikriz.php](https://www.maqamworld.com/en/maqam/nikriz.php)*

🎻 **Violin note:** The augmented 2nd (E♭→F♯) here uses the *same* left-hand stretch you practiced in Jins Hijaz (Module 2) — treat Nikriz as "Hijaz's more dramatic cousin."

## Module 6: Reading the Tradition — Arabic Note Names & Transposition

### 📌 Quick Reference

| Note Name | Approx. Pitch (C-reference) | Note Name | Approx. Pitch |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Yakah** | G (below Rast) | **Nawa** | G |
| **Ushayran** | A (below Rast) | **Husayni** | A |
| **‘Iraq** | B𝄳 (below Rast) | **Awj** | B𝄳 |
| **Rast** | C | **Kirdan** | C (octave) |
| **Dukah** | D | **Muhayyar** | D (above octave) |
| **Sikah** | E𝄳 | **Jiharkah** | F |

*(This is the traditional "spine" of note names used throughout maqamworld and most Arabic theory texts — memorize this before browsing outside sources, since they rarely use letter names.)*

### 6.1 Why Arabic Theory Doesn't Just Use "C, D, E..."

So far, this course has used Western letter names (C, D, E𝄳...) as a teaching convenience — mapping **Rast to C** to keep things simple. But if you go looking at **maqamworld.com** or most authoritative Arabic theory sources, you'll notice something: **they almost never use letter names at all.** Instead, they use a separate naming system — one name per scale degree, regardless of key.

There are two real reasons for this, and understanding them will make the rest of your reading of primary sources far easier:

1. **Historical pitch wasn't standardized.** Unlike Western A440 tuning, traditional Arabic pitch varied by region and era. A note name like "Rast" identifies a *scale-degree role*, not a fixed frequency — so it stayed meaningful even as absolute pitch drifted or varied city to city.
2. **The names double as maqam names.** Notice that *Rast*, *Sikah*, *Nawa*, and *Husayni* are already familiar to you — they're the same words you know as **maqam names**. That's not a coincidence: each note is named after the maqam most closely associated with starting on it. This dual meaning is baked into the entire tradition.

### 6.2 The Note-Name "Spine"

Reading bottom to top, the core sequence of names (using our C-reference, exactly as this course has used since Module 1) is:

```
Yakah — Ushayran — 'Iraq — Rast — Dukah — Sikah — Jiharkah — Nawa — Husayni — Awj — Kirdan — Muhayyar
    G        A        B𝄳      C       D       E𝄳       F         G        A       B𝄳       C         D
```

Notice the pattern repeats after an octave: **Yakah and Nawa are both "G,"** just an octave apart; **Rast and Kirdan are both "C."** This mirrors how, in Western theory, "C" repeats every octave — except here, each octave-repetition also gets its *own distinct name*, because in practice, melodies behave differently depending on which octave they're in (a concept you already saw in Module 3, where **Muhayyar** — the octave above Rast — was mentioned as a distinct melodic zone in **Maqam Bayati**'s variant, "Maqam Muhayyar").

> 🎻 **Violin mapping (using our C-reference):** Rast (C) sits comfortably as the note found on the **G string, 3rd finger**. Dukah (D) is your **open D string**. Confirm this mapping against your actual tuning before performance, since it shifts depending on which string you choose as your starting reference.

### 6.3 Transposition: Same Shape, Different Note

Here's the payoff. Because Arabic maqamat are defined by their **interval pattern** (the quarter-tone formula), not by a fixed pitch, **any maqam can be performed starting on a different note while keeping its identity** — as long as the internal intervals stay the same. This is called **transposition**.

This matters immensely in real performance:
- A singer's vocal range might require **Maqam Bayati** to start on **G (Nawa)** instead of its "home" note **D (Dukah)**.
- An ensemble might retune collectively to fit a particular instrument's open strings.
- The *maqam's name and character stay the same* — only the reference pitch shifts.

#### Example: Maqam Bayati, Transposed

| Version | Tonic | Scale |
|---|---|---|
| **Home form** (as taught in Module 3–4) | Dukah (D) | D – E𝄳 – F – G – A – B♭ – C – D |
| **Transposed up a 5th** | Nawa (G) | G – A𝄳 – B♭ – C – D – E♭ – F – G |

Both versions are still "Maqam Bayati" — same intervallic fingerprint, different starting pitch. A performer or theorist refers to this as **"Bayati on Nawa"** rather than needing a whole new maqam name.

> 📐 **Key insight:** This is *exactly* analogous to transposing a Western melody from C major to G major — the notes change, but "major scale" stays the definition. The difference is that Arabic theory has a **dedicated name for each transposition-starting-point** (Rast, Dukah, Nawa...) instead of relying purely on letter names + key signatures.

🎻 **Practice tip:** Once you're comfortable with Maqam Bayati "on D" (1st position, D string), try transposing it up a 5th to start "on G" — this is an excellent left-hand exercise, since it forces you to relocate the *same finger pattern* to a new string/position rather than relying on memorized note names.

📚 *For a note-name reference chart with audio: [maqamworld.com/en/notes.php](https://www.maqamworld.com/en/notes.php)*

## Module 7: Maqam Families — The Big Map

### 📌 Quick Reference

| Family | Lower Jins | Root Note (home form) | Mnemonic Letter |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Rast** | Jins Rast | C | ر |
| **Bayati** | Jins Bayati | D | ب |
| **Hijaz** | Jins Hijaz | D | ح |
| **Nahawand** | Jins Nahawand | C | ن |
| **Kurd** | Jins Kurd | D | ك |
| **Sikah** | Jins Sikah | E𝄳 | س |
| **Ajam** | Jins Ajam | B♭ (typically) | ع |
| **Saba** | Jins Saba | D | ص |

*(Two new ajnas — Kurd and Saba — are introduced in this module; see 7.2 and 7.3.)*

### 7.1 What Makes a "Family"?

Recall from Module 3: every Maqam is built from a **lower jins** + an **upper jins**. The lower jins is what gives the maqam its fundamental color and stability — it's also what the *tonic* sits on. Traditional theory groups all maqamat that **share the same lower jins** into a single **family**.

**Why this matters practically:**
- Maqamat in the same family are the most natural, idiomatic **modulation partners** — a performer moving from one to another within the same family barely has to "leave home," since the bottom half of the scale doesn't change.
- If you know a family's lower jins cold, you already have a head start on every maqam in that family — only the upper jins (and connection type) differs.
- This is *exactly* the logic behind Module 4's "swap the upper jins, keep the lower jins" modulation technique — family membership is what makes that technique work so smoothly.

#### 🧠 The Memory Aid

Arabic music students traditionally memorize the eight most fundamental ajnas/families using the phrase:

> **صنع بسحرك** (*ṣuniʿa bi-siḥirka*) — "made with your magic"

Each of its 8 Arabic letters stands for one family, in this order: **Ṣabā, Nahāwand, ʿAjam, Bayātī, Sīkāh, Ḥijāz, Rāst, Kurd.**

You've now met **all eight** of these across Modules 2, 5, and this module.

### 7.2 New Jins: Kurd

| Degree | Note | Interval from previous | Quarter-tones |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | D | — | — |
| 2 | E♭ | Semitone | 2 |
| 3 | F | Whole tone | 4 |
| 4 | G | Whole tone | 4 |

**Character:** Resembles the bottom half of the Western **Phrygian mode** (semitone first, then two whole tones). Somber, grounded — a close cousin of Nahawand, but with the "minor" color arriving immediately at the 2nd degree rather than waiting.

🎻 **Violin fingering (D string, 1st position):** 1st finger for the close E♭ (right next to the open D — a small, tight half-step shift), then 2nd and 3rd fingers land normally on F and G.

### 7.3 New Jins: Saba

| Degree | Note | Interval from previous | Quarter-tones |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | D | — | — |
| 2 | E𝄳 | 3/4 tone | 3 |
| 3 | F | 3/4 tone | 3 |
| 4 | G♭ | Semitone | 2 |

**Character:** Unlike every other jins so far, Saba's total span (D to G♭) is *narrower* than a perfect 4th — a genuinely unusual construction. There is unusually broad agreement among performers that Saba's mood is one of **intense yearning or distress**; it's often considered one of the most emotionally striking colors in the entire system.

⚠️ **Advanced note:** Saba's exact tuning and continuation vary more between regional traditions than almost any other jins/maqam. Treat the numbers above as a *teaching approximation* — before performing Saba seriously, cross-check tuning against a trusted recording or your instructor's regional tradition.

### 7.4 The Eight Families at a Glance

| Family | Lower Jins (root) | Notable members |
|---|---|---|
| **Rast** | Rast (C) | Maqam Rast, Suznak, Nairuz, Yakah, Mahur |
| **Bayati** | Bayati (D) | Maqam Bayati, Husayni, Bayati Shuri, Muhayyar |
| **Hijaz** | Hijaz (D) | Maqam Hijaz, Hijazkar, Shahnaz, Shadd ‘Araban, Zanjaran |
| **Nahawand** | Nahawand (C) | Maqam Nahawand, Farahfaza, Nahawand Murassa‘ |
| **Kurd** | Kurd (D) | Maqam Kurd, Hijazkar Kurd, Lami |
| **Sikah** | Sikah (E𝄳) | Maqam Sikah, Huzam, ‘Iraq, Bastanikar, Musta‘ar |
| **Ajam** | Ajam (B♭) | Maqam Ajam, Shawq Afza |
| **Saba** | Saba (D) | Maqam Saba, Saba Zamzam |

> 🔎 **Teaching note:** You'll notice **four different families** (Bayati, Hijaz, Kurd, Saba) all root on the same note-name, **Dukah (D)**. This is completely normal — families are grouped by *jins shape*, not by starting pitch. Four maqamat can all start on D and sound completely different, because it's the *interval pattern above D* that defines which family each belongs to. This is a common point of confusion for beginners — worth sitting with until it clicks.

📚 *Browse each family directly: [maqamworld.com/en/maqam.php](https://www.maqamworld.com/en/maqam.php) (family links appear on each maqam's page)*

### 7.5 Why This Matters for Modulation (Connecting Back to Module 4)

Recall **Tanjees** and **Intiqal** from Module 4. Now you can be much more precise about *where* modulation naturally happens:

- **Modulating within a family** (e.g., Maqam Rast → Maqam Suznak, both rooted in Jins Rast) usually only requires changing the **upper** jins — smooth, common, and idiomatic.
- **Modulating across families** (e.g., Maqam Rast → Maqam Hijaz) requires changing the **lower** jins itself — a much bigger, more structurally significant shift, typically reserved for major formal transitions in a piece rather than passing color.

This distinction — same-family vs. cross-family modulation — is one of the most useful analytical tools you now have for understanding *why* a piece of Arabic music moves the way it does.

## Module 8: Survey of Common Maqamat

| **Nahawand** | Nahawand | C–D–E♭–F–G–A♭–B♭–C | Somber, "Arabic minor" |
| **Kurd** | Kurd | D–E♭–F–G–A♭–B♭–C–D | Grounded, Phrygian-like |

### 8.1 Maqam Bayati — The Most Beloved Maqam in the Repertory

| Degree | Note | Jins role |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | D | Root of **Jins Bayati** (lower) |
| 2 | E𝄳 | |
| 3 | F | |
| 4 | G | Top of lower jins — **shared note** |
| 4/5 | G | Root of upper jins — **Ghammaz** (**Ittisal**) |
| 5 | A | |
| 6 | B♭ | |
| 7 | C | |
| 8 | D | Octave — home |

**Full scale:** D – E𝄳 – F – G – A – B♭ – C – D

**Structure:** Jins Bayati (D) + Ittisal (shared G) + **Jins Nahawand** (G) = Maqam Bayati

**Character:** Bayati is arguably the single most performed maqam across the Arab world — used constantly in folk song, dabke, religious chant, and popular music alike. Its opening neutral-2nd step (D→E𝄳) gives it an immediately recognizable "wistful" quality.

> 🔎 **Related maqam:** **Maqam Husayni** uses the *exact same lower Jins Bayati*, but is analyzed with a different emphasis pattern on the 5th degree — in many folk traditions, this is actually the more commonly heard everyday form.

🎻 **Violin mapping:** Comfortable in 1st position starting on the **open D string**. The E𝄳 (1st finger, slightly forward of natural E) is the single most important pitch to drill here — it's the emotional core of the entire maqam.

📚 *[maqamworld.com/en/maqam/bayati.php](https://www.maqamworld.com/en/maqam/bayati.php)*

### 8.2 Maqam Nahawand — The "Arabic Minor"

| Degree | Note | Jins role |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | C | Root of **Jins Nahawand** (lower) |
| 2 | D | |
| 3 | E♭ | |
| 4 | F | Top of lower jins |
| — | *(whole tone gap: F → G)* | **Infisal** |
| 5 | G | Root of upper jins — **Ghammaz** |
| 6 | A♭ | |
| 7 | B♭ | |
| 8 | C | Octave — home |

**Full scale:** C – D – E♭ – F – G – A♭ – B♭ – C

**Structure:** Jins Nahawand (C) + Infisal (whole-tone gap) + **Jins Kurd** (G) = Maqam Nahawand

**Character:** The maqam closest in sound to the Western natural minor scale, containing no quarter-tones at all — a very approachable entry point after the quarter-tone-heavy Rast/Bayati/Sikah family. Frequently used for emotionally weighty, reflective material.

🎻 **Violin mapping:** Playable entirely with standard Western minor-scale fingerings — an excellent "confidence" maqam to perform early, since your existing scale technique transfers almost directly.

📚 *[maqamworld.com/en/maqam/nahawand.php](https://www.maqamworld.com/en/maqam/nahawand.php)*

### 8.3 Maqam Kurd — Grounded and Direct

| Degree | Note | Jins role |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | D | Root of **Jins Kurd** (lower) |
| 2 | E♭ | |
| 3 | F | |
| 4 | G | Top of lower jins — **shared note** |
| 4/5 | G | Root of upper jins — **Ghammaz** (**Ittisal**) |
| 5 | A♭ | |
| 6 | B♭ | |
| 7 | C | |
| 8 | D | Octave — home |

**Full scale:** D – E♭ – F – G – A♭ – B♭ – C – D

**Structure:** Jins Kurd (D) + Ittisal (shared G) + **Jins Kurd** (G) = Maqam Kurd

**Character:** Like Maqam Rast (Module 3), this maqam is built from **the same jins twice** — giving it a strong internal symmetry. The immediate semitone at the very start (D→E♭) gives it a more grounded, "settled" quality compared to Bayati's softer neutral 2nd.

🎻 **Violin mapping:** The tight D→E♭ half-step at the very start of the piece is the main technical focus — practice landing this interval cleanly before attempting full-tempo passages.

Performers and theorists broadly agree it carries the most intense emotional character of the entire system: grief, longing, distress. Because its scale doesn't behave symmetrically octave-to-octave (note that the top note is D♭, not a natural D), it genuinely breaks the "just repeat the pattern" intuition you've built so far — a useful reminder that not every maqam fits a tidy formula.

🎻 **Violin mapping:** The G♭ (narrower interval than usual) requires careful ear-training — this is not a note you can "guess" from standard fingering patterns. Isolate it with a drone or tuner before attempting melodic passages.
